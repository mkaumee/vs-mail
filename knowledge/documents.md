# Bills of lading: the lifecycle

## Draft to release

1. The shipper sends a **shipping instruction** (SI) — what should appear on
   the bill of lading.
2. The carrier or forwarder types a **draft BL** from it.
3. The draft comes back for **verification** against the SI.
4. Confirmed, the carrier **issues** the BL.
5. The BL is released — as originals, a seawaybill, or by telex release.

Steps 3 and 4 are where this system does its work. Nothing is corrected after
step 4 without a fee and a delay.

## Why the draft is checked

Once issued, a bill of lading is a **document of title**. Whoever holds it can
claim the cargo. Correcting an issued BL means an amendment request to the
carrier, an amendment fee, and — if the manifest has already been filed with
customs at destination — a manifest correction as well. If the vessel has
sailed, more again.

The seven fields that are checked are the ones that cost the most when wrong:
shipper, consignee, notify party, port of loading, port of discharge,
container count, gross weight.

## Requests for a draft

"Please assist to send the draft BL for <reference> for checking" asks us to
produce and send a draft — an action, not a question. The reply acknowledges
it and says when the draft will follow, or asks for the shipping instruction
if we do not have one.

## Cut-offs

A draft BL sits against a **documentation cut-off**, usually one to two
working days before the vessel departs, and earlier than the cargo cut-off.
Miss it and the choice is to sail with an uncorrected BL and amend later, or
roll to the next sailing.

Verified gross mass (VGM) has its own cut-off, and a container without a
submitted VGM is not loaded at all.

## Telex release

Releases the cargo at destination without an original BL travelling there.
Used when the vessel will arrive before the paperwork could. It needs all
originals surrendered at origin first, and carries its own fee.
